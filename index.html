What Am I Looking At?

A locational marginal price (LMP) is the price of delivering one more megawatt-hour of electricity to a specific point on the grid. It moves with the time of day. Power is cheap overnight and climbs with the morning ramp. At midday, solar can flood the grid and push prices below zero. In the evening the sun sets just as demand peaks, and prices spike. Grid operators call this shape the “duck curve.”

The example data is simulated and deliberately exaggerated so the shape is easy to see. Each dot is one price observation at a random time of day, taken over many days. Some evenings the peak is tame and some it is brutal, so the dots spread out around the spike.

The network gets a single input, the hour of day (X1), and has to learn the price from it. Press play and watch the black line, which is the network’s prediction, bend from a flat guess into the duck curve. Each hidden neuron learns a simple piece of the curve, like a step up or a step down at some hour, and the output layer adds the pieces together.

Things to Try

  • Remove neurons until the network can no longer fit the evening spike.
  • Switch the activation to ReLU and watch the prediction turn into straight line segments.
  • Add the X12 or sin(X1) features and compare how fast the loss drops.
  • Turn on “Show test data”, then try to make the network overfit: use less training data and more neurons until the test loss climbs above the training loss.
  • Click the Custom thumbnail to type a formula or paste a CSV of your own prices.

What Do All the Colors Mean?

Orange shows low values and blue shows high values.

In the output plot, each dot’s height and color show its price. The background shows the price the network predicts at each hour, and the black line traces the same prediction.

In the network, the lines are colored by the weights of the connections between neurons. Blue is a positive weight and orange is a negative one; thicker lines are larger weights. Each neuron’s small square shows what that neuron outputs across the hours of the day.
